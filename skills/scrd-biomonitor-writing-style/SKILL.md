# Sunshine Coast Biomonitoring Portal — Style Guide

Source reviewed: **every page on the site** — Home, Biodiversity Research (all 12 stories), People, Get Involved, Natural History, Additional Opportunities, Data Repositories, Contact, and all 13 Organizations pages (Hotel Lake Advisory Association, Loon Foundation, Sargeant Bay Society, Sunshine Coast Natural History Society, Sunshine Coast Salmonid Enhancement Society, Sunshine Coast Streamkeepers Society, Sunshine Coast Friends of Forage Fish, Pender Harbour Wildlife Society, Sunshine Coast Wildlife Project, Sunshine Coast Bear Alliance, Sunshine Coast Conservation Association, BlueAct Marine Society, Wild Ocean Whale Society).

Biodiversity Research section re-reviewed against the live page in October 2026, after the site owner's rewrites of the Sand Lance, Swainson's Thrush, and Oystercatcher stories and a round of line edits across the others. Those edits are the best evidence of the intended voice; Part 3 summarizes what they removed.

## PART 1: General Site Style Guide

### Voice & Tone
- **Plain, warm, and civic-minded.** Writes like a knowledgeable local naturalist explaining something to neighbours, not a government report, a press release, or a marketing brochure.
- **Community-first.** Non-profits, volunteers, and named local people are consistently centred and credited. The site never treats "the science" as separate from "the people collecting it."
- **Honest, not alarmist.** Acknowledges declines, threats, and uncertainty (e.g., salmon monitoring declines, invasive species) and usually circles back to what local people and organizations are doing about it.
- Second person is used sparingly and only in calls to action ("Go to their websites…", "Reach out to any of the organizations…") or one light aside per story at most ("The sea star you photographed at low tide in Davis Bay…").
- Light, dry humour occasionally appears in quoted material (e.g., "If I had to choose the top 17…") but not in the narrator's own voice.
- On utility pages (Get Involved, Natural History, Additional Opportunities, Data Repositories, Contact), the tone is **plain, efficient, and directive**: shorter sentences, more bullets. Story pages (Biodiversity Research, People) allow more explanation but read like plain field reports written for neighbours, not magazine features.

### Structure & Formatting Conventions
- **Clear H1 page title**, short framing sentence/subtitle under it.
- **H2 for major sections**, occasional **H3** for sub-themes within a long section (e.g., "More than Biomonitoring," "Data and Methods").
- **Organization directory pages follow a strict, confirmed template**, verified across all 13 org pages:
  1. A short founding/mission blurb, usually a **direct quote** from the org's own website (marked "Quoted from..." or with attributed quotation marks), occasionally supplemented with one sentence of independent framing.
  2. A fixed set of bolded field labels, always in this order:
     - **Monitoring Programs:** — "Yes" / "No" / "No known current programs" followed by a bulleted list where applicable, or a sentence describing the program(s) when there's just one or two.
     - **Education Programs:** — Yes/No plus a brief description.
     - **Monitoring and Community Science Volunteer Opportunities:** — Yes/No plus how to inquire.
     - **Website:**, **Location:**, **Contact:** — always in that order, as a labeled mini-list.
  3. Two buttons/links at the bottom: **Data Repository** and **Monitoring Methods**, each pointing to a dedicated sub-page named `[org-slug]-data-repository.html` and `[org-slug]-methods.html`.
  - Labels use **"Monitoring," not "Biomonitoring"** ("Monitoring Programs," "Monitoring Methods"). "BioMonitoring Methods" survives only in the `suggestion-*.html` mockups, which are not live pages. "Biomonitoring" is still fine in running prose and in the site name.
  - This template is followed with zero deviation across all org pages. Even orgs with "No" answers to every field (e.g., Sunshine Coast Bear Alliance, Wild Ocean Whale Society) keep the exact same structure rather than omitting empty fields. **When drafting or editing an org page, treat this template as fixed, not a loose guideline.**
- Takeaway callouts on story pages use exactly two labels: **Key Findings** and **Key Takeaways** (see Part 2, §5).
- Bulleted lists are used for enumerable things (programs, species, sites, guides), not for narrative content.
- The **Natural History** and **Data Repositories** pages use a lightweight repeating micro-template: a resource name as the heading, a bare link on its own line, then a one- or two-sentence description. Closer to an annotated bibliography than prose.
- Consistent header/footer navigation and a repeated tagline: *"Biodiversity Monitoring, Education & Research on the Sunshine Coast, BC."* The nav label for `biodiversity-research.html` is "Biodiversity Research" sitewide (it was previously "Current Research"/"Research Stories," so watch for stray old labels when editing older content).
- Site is branded at the very bottom footer as **R2 Foundation**.

### Language & Mechanics
- **Spelling:** Canadian -our endings (colour, behaviour, favour, neighbouring) and -ize endings (organization, characterize), plus Canadian forms like modelling, armouring, centre. **Exception, by the site owner's choice: single-l "fueled / fueling" and "traveling."** Do not "correct" these to fuelled/travelling.
- **Place names:** write **"BC"**, never "B.C." Write **"Pacific Northwest"** (capitalized). **"the Coast"** with a capital C means the Sunshine Coast; lowercase "coast" means coastline in general.
- **Numbers:** use numerals for data and counts, and **add a comma from 1,000 up** (1,000; 7,000; 122,000). Years, page numbers, and article IDs take no comma. Leave numbers inside direct quotations exactly as the source wrote them. Spelled-out small numbers are fine in casual narrative ("two of Krkošek's graduate students").
- **Species names:** common name first, with the *italicized scientific (binomial) name* in parentheses or apposition on first use, e.g., "Sea lice, *Lepeophtheirus salmonis*…"
- **People:** first mention gets the full name, role/affiliation, and a hyperlink to a bio, lab, or People-page entry where one exists. Names are **linked, not bolded**.
- Organizations are hyperlinked at first mention in a piece. Org pages quote directly from the org's own website for mission/founding language, always attributed ("Quoted from the Society's website").
- **"Citizen science" and "community science"** are both acceptable and both appear on the site. No need to standardize.
- **No em dashes (—) in new narrative writing.** Use commas, parentheses, colons, or separate sentences. En dashes (–) for ranges like "2014–2023" or page ranges are fine. An em dash inside a direct quotation stays as the source wrote it.
- First Nations territory and place names are acknowledged with specific, correctly spelled nation names (Sḵwx̱wú7mesh, shíshálh, Tla'amin, Klahoose, Homalco). Precision and respect here is a fixed convention, not optional flavour text. This acknowledgment reappears near-verbatim on the Home page and the Sunshine Coast Conservation Association page, so reuse it rather than rewording it.
- Contact info for organizations is handled flexibly: sometimes a direct email, sometimes "See website," sometimes a contact-page URL. Follow whatever the organization itself provides.

### Sourcing & Citations
- Full academic citations appear at the end of stories. House format for new citations:
  `Surname, I. et al. Year. Title. *Journal* Volume: pages.`
  - No comma between "et al." and the year. Journal name in italics. Scientific names in titles italicized.
  - Existing citations on the page are not yet fully consistent (e.g., "Gow et al., 2008," and the Huard and Lovas-Kiss journal names aren't italicized). Follow the house format for new work rather than copying an inconsistent neighbour.
- External links are inline, embedded in relevant nouns/phrases, not "click here" style. Occasional "here" links for reports or news articles are fine.
- Photo/figure captions include the photographer or source credit and licensing where relevant (e.g., "CC BY 2.0", Wikimedia Commons attributions).
- Resource/guide listings (Natural History, Data Repositories pages) cite author names plainly next to the resource title, without full academic formatting.

### Recurring Visual/Content Elements
- Featured header images per page.
- Figures/photos embedded with captions crediting the source study or photographer.
- A category tag sits above each H2 on the Biodiversity Research page: **"Coast Monitoring Fuels Science"** or **"Research Story"** (see Part 2).

### Site-wide Navigation & Cross-linking Pattern
- Every page carries the same nav bar, in this order: **Home, Organizations, People, Biodiversity Research, Natural History, Get Involved, Additional Opportunities, Data Repositories, Contact**. The footer repeats the same links (Contact sits in the footer brand block rather than the footer link list).
- Heavy internal cross-linking is deliberate: org pages link to relevant Biodiversity Research stories; stories link back to org pages and People profiles; Get Involved links out to Organizations, Additional Opportunities, and Biodiversity Research. New content should link back into this web wherever a natural connection exists.

## PART 2: Biodiversity Research Module — Story Style Guide

**Stories on the page: 12**

1. Monitoring for Fisheries, or for Fish?
2. Counting Lice: Tracking a Tiny Threat to Wild Salmon
3. iNaturalist on the Coast
4. Birds on the move up Tetrahedron
5. Shining a Light on Dungeness Crab Larvae
6. Seabirds on the Move Along BC's Coast
7. How did Sunfish get into Trout and Colvin Lakes?
8. Stickleback and the Origin of Species on the Sunshine Coast
9. Mapping Pacific Sand Lance Spawning Habitat in the Salish Sea
10. Two Roads South: The Divided Migration of Swainson's Thrushes
11. Citizen Science at Hotel Lake: Tracing the Diet of an Invasive Jellyfish
12. Tracking Oystercatchers Reveals Habitat Choices That Trade Off Foraging & Avoiding Predators

Use this section as the reference whenever drafting a **new** Biodiversity Research story or editing an existing one.

### 1. Story Length & Shape
- Typically **400–650 words** of body text, rarely over 800 (current range is about 320–790; most sit between 420 and 620).
- Built from **4–7 short-to-medium paragraphs**, rarely more than 5–6 sentences each.
- Ends with a **"Key Findings" or "Key Takeaways" callout**: one tight paragraph (2–5 sentences), or two short ones, that a reader could get value from even if they skipped everything above it.
- Full citation(s) follow the callout, on their own line(s).
- A category tag line sits directly above the H2 title. Use **"Coast Monitoring Fuels Science"** when local volunteer/monitoring data directly feeds the research discussed. Use **"Research Story"** when the piece is about a general or adjacent finding with a looser local tie-in.

### 2. Opening (Hook Paragraph)
- Opens with a **plain, concrete statement of fact**: what the organism or phenomenon is and does, a piece of history, or a clear question. Establish stakes before introducing the researchers or the paper.
  - Current examples: *"The Swainson's Thrush is a small, spotted songbird that breeds across the boreal forests of North America and winters in Central and South America."* / *"Black oystercatchers, the orange-billed shorebirds of our rocky shorelines, are considered an indicator of a healthy intertidal zone."* / *"In summer, as the water in Hotel Lake warms past 21°C, thousands of pale, coin-sized jellyfish drift through the shallows."*
- Avoid opening with "A recent study found…". The exception is a story built around a single paper or platform, where naming it up front is the hook (iNaturalist: "A recent paper by Mason et al., … makes a strong case…").
- A common closing move for the hook paragraph is to name the gap the research fills ("…until recently knowledge of their spawning habitats was limited…", "Much less is known about what oystercatchers do the rest of the year…").

### 3. Middle (Research Narrative)
- Introduces the **lead researcher(s) by name, linked** to a lab or university page where one exists, with their institution. Often names local collaborators, students, and organizations too.
- Explains **methodology in plain language**, enough for a lay reader to picture what was actually done (e.g., "walking trails, stopping at a series of set stations, and listening to calls," "seine netting and enumerating juvenile salmon," "light-level geolocators").
- Explains a relevant concept, hypothesis, or background before or alongside the findings (the three mountain-shift hypotheses, migratory allopatry, stable isotope analysis, MaxEnt).
- **Always draws the explicit local connection**: how this ties to the Sunshine Coast, a local non-profit, a named local biomonitor, or local data. This is the non-negotiable core of the module and must appear even if the study is regional or global.
  - The pivot to the local angle often gets its own short lead paragraph (`<p class="story-lead">`): *"Here is a recent local study of birds on the move…"* / *"Two local sites, Pender Harbour and Gibsons, are included in this study…"* / *"Here on the Sunshine Coast, this global platform has been made local…"* / *"Local efforts are picking up much of the slack on the Coast."*
- Numbers and stats are used precisely (percentages, counts, years, sample sizes) and always explained in context.
- One embedded **figure or photo** is typical, with a caption crediting the source paper or photographer, including licensing where relevant.
- Bold subheads (a `<p><strong>…</strong></p>`, not an H3) are used occasionally inside longer stories with two or more distinct threads. Keep them plain and descriptive: *"Citizen Science Fuels Habitat Modelling," "What is a Suitable Habitat for Sand Lance Spawning."*
- An optional **"Updates:"** paragraph (bold lead-in) can sit just before the callout to note newer related work. A separate `story-update` box is used for news developments after publication (see the Monitoring for Fisheries story).

### 4. Tone Within Stories
- Explanatory and plain, like a knowledgeable neighbour describing a study. Not a press release, not a dry abstract, not a magazine feature.
- **Match the confidence of the research.** Prefer "suggest," "may," "can," "likely" unless the paper is definitive. The site owner's edits consistently soften overclaims: "The results show" → "The results suggest"; "is not enough" → "may not be enough"; "could affect" → "may affect"; "is passed along to GBIF" → "can be passed along to GBIF."
- **Say the literal thing.** "Populations that are neighbours during the breeding season have little or no contact for the rest of the year," not "lead separate lives."
- Avoid jargon without explanation. When a technical term is needed (eDNA, MaxEnt, stable isotope analysis, migratory allopatry), define it in-line immediately.
- Name conservation stakes plainly, without preaching.
- A rhetorical question is acceptable once in a story when it genuinely frames the puzzle ("So how did the sunfish get into Colvin Lake?"). Don't use one as a dramatic transition ("Why should this be a concern?" was rewritten as "There is reason for concern.").
- **No literary or poetic devices.** No personification, dramatic scene-painting, or metaphor (a "countdown" beginning, "something ancient stirs," a species "racing" a season, birds that "patrol" a shoreline).
- No evaluative flourishes in the narrator's voice ("a quiet but serious vulnerability," "the feast has already passed," "remarkable," "striking"). State the finding and its implication plainly and let the facts carry the weight.
- If a draft reads more like a magazine feature than a field report, it has drifted too far. When in doubt, cut adjectives and metaphor rather than add them.

### 5. Closing (Callout + Citation)
- The callout label is exactly **"Key Findings"** or **"Key Takeaways"**, with no trailing colon or period. Use "Key Findings" when the callout reports results; use "Key Takeaways" when it is about what the work means for the Coast, monitoring, or citizen science. Markup:
  ```html
  <div class="key-finding">
      <strong class="key-finding-label">Key Findings</strong>
      …
  </div>
  ```
- The callout restates the finding **and** its significance or implication, not just a summary.
- Full academic citation(s) follow directly, in the house format from Part 1.
- If a story draws on several related papers, list them all, oldest or most foundational first (see the Swainson's Thrush story for a three-citation example).

### 6. Linking Conventions for Stories
- Local organizations: hyperlink at first mention to their page on this site.
- Local people: hyperlink at first mention to their entry on the People page (anchor link, e.g. `local-biomonitors.html#jenn-blancard`).
- Researchers/labs/institutions: hyperlink to their official lab or university bio page.
- External articles/news coverage: hyperlink inline via a descriptive phrase or "here," not raw URLs (except source repositories, which may show as a bare link, e.g. `https://sentinels.hakai.org/`).

## PART 3: Keep Signs of AI Writing to a Minimum

The site owner rewrites drafts that read as machine-generated. These are the patterns they have removed most often. Check every draft against this list before handing it over.

- **Em dashes.** None in new narrative text (see Part 1). This is the most recognizable tell.
- **Reflexive triplets.** Avoid automatic lists of three ("salmon, seabirds, and whales"; "evolution, ecology, and conservation"; "warm, rich, and vital"). Use three items only when there are genuinely three things to list. Two or four is fine, and so is one.
- **Punchy setup sentences.** No short dramatic lines that exist only to tee up the next sentence: "That gap is starting to close." / "The outflow finding was unexpected." / "Why should this be a concern?" / "It also got big." Fold the point into a full sentence instead.
- **Hype and intensifier words.** Avoid remarkable, striking, critically, crucially, keystone, cornerstone, world-famous, dramatic, vital, pivotal, groundbreaking, fascinating, stand-out, incredibly. Edits on the page: "a keystone forage fish" → "a key forage fish"; "a cornerstone of coastal Indigenous life" → "important to coastal Indigenous life"; "the world-famous threespine stickleback" → "the threespine stickleback"; "This stand-out collaboration" → "This close collaboration."
- **Chatty signposts and filler openers.** Avoid "Here's how it works," "Basically," "Critically," "Notably," "Importantly," "Interestingly," "It's worth noting." Edit on the page: "Here's how it works. You upload a photo, and…" → "Users can upload their photos and…"
- **Idioms and stock metaphors.** Avoid knock-on effects, sweet spot, picking up the slack, a window into, sheds new light, paints a picture, a perfect storm, game-changer, lead separate lives. Say the literal thing.
- **Contrast templates.** Avoid "not just X, but Y," "X isn't about A, it's about B," and "It's not X. It's Y."
- **Colon reveals and dramatic lists.** Avoid "The picture that emerges: …" or "…trophic levels: more phytoplankton, a greener lake, and reduced fish production." Write it as an ordinary sentence ("…trophic levels, including more phytoplankton and hence a greener lake, and reduced fish production").
- **Overclaiming.** Don't state as fact what the paper only suggests (see Part 2, §4).
- **Restating closers.** Don't end a paragraph by repeating its own point ("This further emphasizes the value of…", "This highlights the importance of…"). Save significance for the callout.
- **Uniform rhythm.** Vary sentence length naturally. A run of same-length sentences, or every paragraph ending on a neat one-line summary, reads as generated.

When in doubt, write the plainer sentence.

### Quick Checklist for a New Story
- [ ] Category tag above title: "Coast Monitoring Fuels Science" or "Research Story"
- [ ] Plain, concrete opening that says what the organism or phenomenon is (not "A study found…" unless the story is about that one paper)
- [ ] Named, linked lead researcher + institution
- [ ] Plain-language explanation of method
- [ ] Explicit Sunshine Coast / local non-profit / local biomonitor tie-in, ideally in its own short lead paragraph
- [ ] Relevant background concept explained in plain terms
- [ ] Optional plain bold subhead(s) if the story has 2+ distinct threads
- [ ] Optional figure/photo with a credited caption
- [ ] "Key Findings" or "Key Takeaways" callout (no trailing punctuation) with implication, not just summary
- [ ] Full academic citation(s) in house format
- [ ] About 400–650 words of body text
- [ ] Hedged to match the research ("suggest," "may")
- [ ] BC (not B.C.), Pacific Northwest, commas from 1,000 up, "fueled"/"traveling"
- [ ] Part 3 pass: no em dashes, no reflexive triplets, no hype words, no punchy setup lines, no idioms or contrast templates
