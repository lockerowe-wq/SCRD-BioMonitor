# Sunshine Coast Biomonitoring Portal — Style Guide

Source reviewed: **every page on the site** — Home, Biodiversity Research (all 12 stories), People, Get Involved, Natural History, Additional Opportunities, Data Repositories, Contact, and all 12 Organizations pages (Hotel Lake Advisory Association, Loon Foundation, Sargeant Bay Society, Sunshine Coast Natural History Society, Sunshine Coast Salmonid Enhancement Society, Sunshine Coast Streamkeepers Society, Sunshine Coast Friends of Forage Fish, Pender Harbour Wildlife Society, Sunshine Coast Wildlife Project, Sunshine Coast Bear Alliance, Sunshine Coast Conservation Association, BlueAct Marine Society, Wild Ocean Whale Society).

## PART 1: General Site Style Guide

### Voice & Tone
- **Warm, civic-minded, and quietly authoritative.** Writes like a knowledgeable local guide, not a government report or a marketing brochure.
- **Community-first.** Non-profits, volunteers, and named local people are consistently centered and credited — the site never treats "the science" as separate from "the people collecting it."
- **Optimistic but honest.** Acknowledges declines, threats, and uncertainty (e.g., salmon monitoring declines, invasive species) without becoming alarmist; always circles back to what local people/organizations are doing about it.
- Second person is used sparingly and only in calls to action ("Go to their websites…", "Reach out to any of the organizations…").
- Light, dry humour occasionally appears in quoted material (e.g., "If I had to choose the top 17…") but never in the narrator's own voice — that stays measured and factual.
- On utility pages (Get Involved, Natural History, Additional Opportunities, Data Repositories, Contact), the tone shifts slightly toward **plain, efficient, and directive** — shorter sentences, more bullets, less narrative colour than the Biodiversity Research stories or People profiles. The site consistently matches register to purpose: storytelling pages read like magazine features; reference/directory pages read like a well-organized library guide.

### Structure & Formatting Conventions
- **Clear H1 page title**, short framing sentence/subtitle under it.
- **H2 for major sections**, occasional **H3** for sub-themes within a long section (e.g., "More than Biomonitoring," "Data and Methods").
- **Organization directory pages follow a strict, confirmed template**, verified across all 12 org pages:
  1. A short founding/mission blurb, usually a **direct quote** from the org's own website (marked "Quoted from..." or with attributed quotation marks), occasionally supplemented with one sentence of independent framing.
  2. A fixed set of bolded field labels, always in this order:
     - **Biomonitoring Programs:** — "Yes" / "No" / "No known current programs" followed by a bulleted list where applicable, or a sentence describing the program(s) when there's just one or two.
     - **Education Programs:** — Yes/No plus a brief description.
     - **Biomonitoring and Community Science Volunteer Opportunities:** — Yes/No plus how to inquire.
     - **Website:**, **Location:**, **Contact:** — always in that order, as a labeled mini-list.
  3. Two buttons/links at the bottom: **Data Repository** and **BioMonitoring Methods**, each pointing to a dedicated sub-page named `[org-slug]-data-repository.html` and `[org-slug]-methods.html`.
  - This template is followed with zero deviation across all 12 orgs — even orgs with "No" answers to every field (e.g., Sunshine Coast Bear Alliance, Wild Ocean Whale Society) keep the exact same structure, rather than omitting empty fields. **When drafting or editing an org page, this template should be treated as fixed, not a loose guideline.**
- Bolded lead-in phrases are used to flag takeaway content: **Key Finding**, **Key Takeaway(s)**, **Takeaways**, **Updates**.
- Bulleted lists used for enumerable things (programs, species, sites, guides) — not for narrative content.
- The **Natural History** and **Data Repositories** pages use a lightweight repeating micro-template of their own: a short category label (e.g., "Birds," "Insects," "Marine") followed by a title/resource name, then author or descriptive line — closer to an annotated bibliography than prose.
- Consistent header/footer navigation and a repeated tagline: *"Biodiversity Monitoring, Education & Research on the Sunshine Coast, BC."* (Note: the nav label for `biodiversity-research.html` is now "Biodiversity Research" sitewide — it was previously renamed from "Current Research"/"Research Stories," so watch for stray old labels if editing older content.)
- Site is branded at the very bottom footer as **R2 Foundation**.

### Language & Mechanics
- Canadian/British spelling: **-our** endings (colour, favour, neighbouring) but **-ize** endings (organization) — matches Canadian English convention, confirmed consistently across org pages and stories alike (e.g., "favourite," "neighbouring watersheds," "organization").
- Species names: common name first mention, followed by *italicized scientific (binomial) name* in parentheses or apposition on first use, e.g., "Sea lice, *Lepeophtheirus salmonis*…"
- First mention of a person: **full name in bold**, followed by role/affiliation and a hyperlink to a bio, lab, or org page where available.
- Organizations are hyperlinked at first mention in a piece; org pages themselves quote directly from the org's own website for mission/founding language, always attributed ("Quoted from the Society's website," "quoted from the group's website").
- Numbers: mix of numerals and spelled-out small numbers is used loosely (not strictly AP or Chicago) — favour numerals for data/counts, words for small casual numbers in narrative flow.
- Em dashes used for appositive/parenthetical asides.
- First Nations territory and place names are acknowledged with specific, correctly spelled nation names (Sḵwx̱wú7mesh, shíshálh, Tla'amin, Klahoose, Homalco) — precision and respect here is a fixed convention, not optional flavour text. This acknowledgment reappears verbatim/near-verbatim on both the Home page and the Sunshine Coast Conservation Association page, suggesting it's a standard block to reuse rather than reword each time.
- Contact info for organizations is handled flexibly: sometimes a direct email, sometimes "See website," sometimes a contact-page URL — follow whatever the organization itself provides rather than forcing a single format.

### Sourcing & Citations
- Full academic citations appear at the end of sections in a consistent format:
  `Author(s), Year. Title. *Journal*, volume: pages.`
- External links are inline, embedded in relevant nouns/phrases (not "click here" style, except occasional "here" links for reports/articles).
- Photo/figure captions are italicized, include photographer/source credit and licensing info where relevant (e.g., "CC BY 2.0", Wikimedia Commons attributions).
- Resource/guide listings (Natural History, Data Repositories pages) cite author names plainly next to the resource title, without full academic formatting — these are recommended reading/tools, not research citations, and are styled accordingly.

### Recurring Visual/Content Elements
- Featured header images per page.
- Figures/photos embedded with italic captions crediting the source study or photographer.
- Repeating section-divider labels appear before certain headers, functioning like a category tag: **"Coast Biomonitoring Fuels Science"** or **"Research Story"** shown just above H2 headers on the Biodiversity Research page (see Part 2).

### Site-wide Navigation & Cross-linking Pattern
- Every page carries the same nav bar (Home, Organizations, People, Get Involved, Biodiversity Research, Natural History, Additional Opportunities, Data Repositories) and the same footer block, reinforcing the site as a unified portal rather than standalone pages.
- Heavy internal cross-linking is a deliberate pattern: org pages link to relevant Biodiversity Research stories about them; Biodiversity Research stories link back to org pages and People profiles; Get Involved links out to Organizations, Additional Opportunities, and Biodiversity Research. New content should link back into this web wherever a natural connection exists, rather than sitting in isolation.

## PART 2: Biodiversity Research Module — Story Style Guide

**Stories read: 12**

1. Monitoring for Fisheries, or for Fish?
2. Counting Lice: Tracking a Tiny Threat to Wild Salmon
3. iNaturalist on the Coast
4. Birds on the move up Tetrahedron
5. Shining a Light on Dungeness Crab Larvae
6. Seabirds on the Move Along BC's Coast
7. How did Sunfish get into Trout and Colvin Lakes?
8. Stickleback and the Origin of Species on the Sunshine Coast
9. Mapping the Nurseries of the Salish Sea: Pacific Sand Lance Spawning Habitat
10. Two Roads South: The Divided Migration of Swainson's Thrushes
11. Citizen Science at Hotel Lake: Tracing the Diet of an Invasive Jellyfish
12. Tracking Oystercatchers Reveals Habitat Choices That Trade Off Foraging & Avoiding Predators

Use this section as the reference whenever drafting a **new** Biodiversity Research story.

### 1. Story Length & Shape
- Typically **600–1,100 words**.
- Built from **4–7 short-to-medium paragraphs** (rarely more than 5–6 sentences each).
- Almost always ends with a **bolded "Key Finding" / "Key Takeaway(s)" / "Takeaways" callout** — one tight paragraph (2–5 sentences) that a reader could get value from even if they skipped everything above it.
- Full citation(s) in academic format immediately follow the takeaway, on their own line(s).
- A category tag line ("Coast Biomonitoring Fuels Science" or "Research Story") sits directly above the H2 title. Use **"Coast Biomonitoring Fuels Science"** when local volunteer/biomonitoring data directly feeds the research discussed; use **"Research Story"** when the piece is more about a general/adjacent scientific finding with a looser or emerging local tie-in.

### 2. Opening (Hook Paragraph)
- Never opens with the study's citation or "A recent study found…"
- Opens instead with a **vivid, concrete, scene-setting or conceptual hook**: a species behaviour, a historical fact, an evocative image, or a puzzle/question.
  - Examples: *"Sea lice… are marine ectoparasites that graze on the skin, muscle, and blood of their hosts."* / *"In summer, as the water in Hotel Lake warms past 21°C, something strange can happen…"*
- The hook establishes stakes or wonder *before* introducing the researchers or the paper.

### 3. Middle (Research Narrative)
- Introduces the **lead researcher(s) by name, bolded and hyperlinked**, with their institution, and often names local collaborating scientists/students/organizations too.
- Explains **methodology in plain language** — enough for a lay reader to picture what was actually done (e.g., "walking trails, stopping at set stations, listening to calls," "seine netting and enumerating juvenile salmon," "light-level geolocators").
- Contextualizes the science: explains a relevant concept, hypothesis, or historical background before or alongside the findings (e.g., the three mountain-shift hypotheses, migratory allopatry, stable isotope analysis).
- **Always draws the explicit local connection** — how does this tie to the Sunshine Coast, a local non-profit, a named local biomonitor, or local data? This is the non-negotiable core of the module's mission and must appear even if the underlying study is regional/global.
- Numbers and stats are used generously and precisely (percentages, counts, years, sample sizes) but always explained in context, never dropped in raw.
- One embedded **figure or photo** is typical, with an italicized caption crediting the source paper or photographer, often including licensing.
- Subheads (**bold, not H3**, e.g., "**A Rare and Patchy Habitat**") are used occasionally inside longer stories to break up dense sections — optional, use when a story has 2+ distinct findings threads.

### 3a. Punctuation Note: No Em Dashes
Although past stories occasionally use em dashes (—) for asides or parenthetical pairs, **new stories should not use em dashes.** Use commas, parentheses, colons, or separate sentences instead to achieve the same effect. (Note: this does not apply to en dashes (–) used for numeric ranges like "2014–2023," which remain fine and are used throughout the site.)

### 4. Tone Within Stories
- Curious and explanatory, like a well-informed friend narrating a discovery — not a press release, not a dry abstract.
- Uses rhetorical questions sparingly to transition ("So how did the sunfish get into Colvin Lake?").
- Acknowledges scientific uncertainty and open questions honestly ("We will probably never know how sunfish got into Trout or Colvin lakes, but…").
- Avoids jargon without explanation; when a technical term is needed (eDNA, MaxEnt, stable isotope analysis, migratory allopatry), it is defined in-line immediately.
- Conservation stakes and implications are usually named plainly, without being preachy.
- **No literary/poetic devices.** Never open with personification, dramatic scene-painting, or metaphor (a "countdown" beginning, "something ancient stirs," an "old synchrony breaking down," a species "racing" a season). The corpus opens with plain, declarative statements of fact instead: *"The Swainson's Thrush is a small, spotted songbird that breeds across the boreal forests of North America…"* / *"Black oystercatchers, the striking orange-billed shorebirds that patrol our rocky shorelines, are considered an indicator of a healthy intertidal zone."* Establish what the organism or phenomenon *is* and *does* before any narrative colour, not the reverse.
- Avoid evaluative flourishes in the narrator's own voice ("a quiet but serious vulnerability," "the feast has already passed"). State the finding and its implication plainly; let the facts carry the weight.
- Bold subheads should be plain and descriptive ("A Rare and Patchy Habitat," "Data and Methods"), not clever or cute ("Sixty-Six Clocks, Not One").
- If a draft reads more like a magazine feature than a field report, it has drifted too far. When in doubt, cut adjectives and metaphor rather than add them.

### 5. Closing (Takeaway + Citation)
- Bolded lead-in: **Key Finding** / **Key Takeaway** / **Key Takeaways** / **Takeaways** — pick ONE per story, applied consistently as a colon-led inline lead ("**Key Finding** Only 5.4% of…") or as its own mini-header. Both patterns appear in the corpus; either is acceptable, but stay internally consistent within a single story.
- The takeaway restates the finding **and** its significance/implication (not just a summary).
- Full academic citation(s) follow directly in this format:
  `Surname, Initials. et al. Year. Title. *Journal*, Volume: pages.`
- If a study has multiple related papers, list them all, most foundational or most recent first — see the Swainson's Thrush story for a 3-citation example.
- Occasionally an **"Updates:"** bolded line appears just before the Key Finding to note newer/related developments from the same research group.

### 6. Linking Conventions for Stories
- Local organizations: hyperlink at first mention to their page on this site.
- Local people: hyperlink at first mention to their entry on the People page (anchor link, e.g. `local-biomonitors.html#jenn-blancard`).
- Researchers/labs/institutions: hyperlink to their official lab or university bio page.
- External articles/news coverage: hyperlink inline via descriptive phrase or "here," not raw URLs (except source repositories, which may show as a bare link e.g. `<https://sentinels.hakai.org/>`).

### Quick Checklist for a New Story
- [ ] Category tag above title: "Coast Biomonitoring Fuels Science" or "Research Story"?
- [ ] Punchy, concrete opening hook (not "A study found…")
- [ ] Named, bolded, linked lead researcher + institution
- [ ] Plain-language explanation of method
- [ ] Explicit Sunshine Coast / local non-profit / local biomonitor tie-in
- [ ] Relevant background concept explained in plain terms
- [ ] Optional bold subhead(s) if story has 2+ distinct threads
- [ ] Optional figure/photo with italic, credited caption
- [ ] Bolded "Key Finding/Takeaway(s)" closing paragraph with implication, not just summary
- [ ] Full academic citation(s) in house format
- [ ] 600–1,100 words total
- [ ] No em dashes used (commas, parentheses, colons, or separate sentences instead)
